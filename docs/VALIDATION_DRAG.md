# Booster descent drag vs Falcon 9 telemetry

Generated by `python -m rlv_sim.validation.drag_fit`. Unpowered descent between entry-burn cutoff and landing-burn ignition (NROL-76, CRS-11, Zuma; webcast data, public domain). Real drag area assumes a 26 t booster (unpublished; +/-15% scales the result 1:1). Sim: default vehicle, entry and landing phases below 40 km.

| Mach | Falcon 9 C_D*A (m^2) | Sim C_D*A (m^2) | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.9-1.2 | 12.3 | 27.1 | 0.45 |
| 1.2-1.5 | 15.0 | 24.8 | 0.60 |
| 1.5-2.0 | 16.2 | 21.4 | 0.76 |
| 2.0-2.5 | 16.8 | 18.9 | 0.89 |

**Fitted `booster_descent_drag_scale` = 0.68** (range 0.58-0.78 for the mass uncertainty).

## Findings

- The sim's descent drag is **too high, most of all near Mach 1** (about 2x at Mach 0.9-1.2, close at Mach 2-2.5). The built-in Cd(Mach) table peaks transonically; the real booster's drag area instead rises with Mach, as expected for a blunt, engines-first body whose base drag dominates.
- **Not adopted as the default.** The landing guidance has been tuned against the higher drag: with the fitted scale (0.68) 100-run Monte Carlo success (seed 42) falls from 94% to 86%. Making the guidance robust to realistic drag is follow-up work; the scale is available as `booster_descent_drag_scale` (e.g. `python -m rlv_sim.campaign --set booster_descent_drag_scale=0.68`).
- A single scale cannot fix the Mach dependence; a descent-specific Cd(Mach) curve (or imported wind-tunnel / CFD data, Settings -> Your Aero Data) is the proper fix.
