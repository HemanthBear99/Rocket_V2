# Fix roll damping, plan on real drag

Vyom's two most consequential defects are model errors, not tuning problems. The first is the roll jet-damping term. Applying `mdot * cg_z^2 * omega` to roll overstates roll damping by roughly **100-750x**. The physically correct roll coefficient depends on the radial positions of the engines, not on the axial distance from the CG to the nozzles. The second is the landing guidance. It plans on drag that the vehicle does not actually get, so ignition comes too late when drag is lower than modeled. The literature's answer is drag-aware guidance (successive convexification, or drag feedforward with an online drag-scale estimate), free or adaptive ignition timing, and planning that holds thrust in reserve. On propellant, SpaceX publishes no reserve figures. The only number found is a forum estimate of about **56 t remaining at MECO for RTLS**. Vyom's fixed 67.5 t sits above it, which is consistent with Vyom's MECO arriving 6-10% early, but the evidence is weak. The Monte Carlo practice is the gap that is easiest to close. NASA campaigns run **1,000-8,000 cases**, disperse aero coefficients, per-engine thrust and altitude-correlated wind profiles, and state results as one-sided confidence bounds. Vyom's 100 runs over 6 scalar parameters can show at best about 97.7% success at 90% confidence. Throughout this report, statements with citations are sourced. Statements labelled *inference* are derivations or engineering judgment that should be checked in the simulator.

## Roll jet damping is overstated by two to three orders of magnitude

**Sourced facts.** Jet damping is the angular momentum the exhaust carries out through the nozzle exit plane. It stabilizes transverse motion when the distance from the CM to the nozzle exit plane exceeds the transverse radius of gyration ([Wikipedia: Jet damping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_damping), citing van der Ha & Janssens, AIAA GNC 2003, and Etkin & Reid). The general variable-mass balance is 0 = dH*/dt + ∫_S ρ h u dS, with h = p × [v_r + ω × p]. The surface flux integral over the exit is the jet term ([Nanjangud & Eke, arXiv:1612.00884](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1612.00884)). Spin (roll) jet damping is treated in the literature as a separate effect from transverse damping ([ARBRL-MR-03052](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA089980.pdf)). Expelling propellant makes the pitch-inertia rate negative ([same](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/ADA089980.pdf)). The stability criterion l > k implies net damping proportional to mdot(l² − k²) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jet_damping)). Among the open simulators checked, JSBSim documents no explicit jet-damping term ([FGMassBalance](https://jsbsim.sourceforge.net/JSBSim/classJSBSim_1_1FGMassBalance.html)). Basilisk handles mass depletion through its fuelTank back-substitution ([fuelTank docs](https://www.hanspeterschaub.info/basilisk/_sources/Documentation/simulation/dynamics/FuelTank/fuelTank.rst.txt)). So Vyom gets no free reference implementation to copy.

**Inferences: derivation and magnitudes.** Evaluate the ω × p part of the flux for each engine i, with its exit centre at p_i = (x_i, y_i, −l) about the current CG. The result is a jet-inertia matrix:

J = Σ_i mdot_i [(p_i·p_i)E − p_i p_iᵀ] + diag(0, 0, Σ_i mdot_i r_e²/2), with τ_damp = −J ω.

Pitch and yaw then come out to about mdot·l². Roll comes out to Σ mdot_i (x_i² + y_i² + r_e²/2). Plug in the assumed geometry: mdot 2,500 kg/s, outer ring radius 1.5 m, exit radius 0.46 m. These inputs are unverified assumptions, not sourced Falcon 9 data.

| Burn | C_roll (kg·m²/s) | C_pitch (kg·m²/s) |
|---|---|---|
| 9 engines | ≈5.3e3 | 5.6e5 (l=15 m) to 4.0e6 (l=40 m) |
| 3 engines (centre + 2) | ≈1.3e3 | ≈833·l² |
| Centre only (landing) | ≈29 | ≈278·l² |

Assume a roll inertia of 1-3e5 kg·m² (unsourced). The current bug then damps roll in **0.03-0.5 s**. The correct time constant is **20-60 s**, and during centre-engine landing burns there is essentially no roll damping at all. Every roll-control result that depends on this term is therefore suspect. That covers RCS/grid-fin roll authority and the roll transients during entry and landing.

**Recommendations, in order.** (1) Replace the scalar term with the J-matrix above, built from the active engine set and lever arms about the *current* CG. Symmetric off-diagonal terms cancel for a full octaweb. They do not cancel for engine-out or subset burns, and the matrix form handles those cases automatically. (2) Audit for double counting. Use either raw mdot·l² together with an explicit İω, or the net coefficient mdot(l² − k²) without İω, never both. The p × v_r part of the flux is the thrust moment, which is already in the gimbal torque. (3) Take İ from the mass-properties model, for example by finite-differencing I about the current CG. Do not assume uniform mass loss, because tanks draining from the bottom give a different pitch İ. Keep the sign convention ω̇ = I⁻¹(τ − ω×Iω − İω − Jω) with İ < 0. (4) Add a unit test with a pure roll rate, centre engine only, and assert that roll decay is negligible. Then compare full-mission telemetry CSVs before and after the change. Before citing equations in the docs, verify them against Thomson's variable-mass chapter or the Basilisk FUELTANK memo. The research did not retrieve either source.

## Landing guidance fails because it plans on drag the vehicle does not get

**Sourced facts.** Lossless convexification (G-FOLD) relaxes minimum-fuel powered descent to an SOCP with a guaranteed global optimum, for drag-free dynamics ([Açıkmeşe & Ploen 2007](https://ece.uwaterloo.ca/~ece602/Projects/2016/Papers/paper9.pdf)). Blackmore, describing SpaceX practice, says that on Earth "aerodynamic forces become the primary concern." He also says the onboard solvers are generated with CVXGEN, that divert trajectories must be solved in a fraction of a second, and that dispersions from winds and atmosphere grow during entry and are removed at boostback and at the landing burn ([NAP ch.10](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/23659/chapter/10)). Successive convexification adds drag ([Szmuk & Açıkmeşe 2016](https://archive.cps-vo.org/node/51778)). The full 6-DoF version adds lift, drag, a free ignition time and state-triggered constraints, with execution times under 0.7 s ([arXiv 1811.10803](https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.10803)). Aerodynamic forces during retropropulsive descent "may be comparable with the commanded thrust," which rules out an exact convex reformulation ([arXiv 2608.15582](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.15582)).

The most directly usable architecture is Model Predictive Guidance ([arXiv 2405.01264](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.01264v1)). Its planner is a 3-DoF SOCP with free ignition time that solves in about 0.2 s and is re-run only when tracking error exceeds 10 m. Its tracker is a 4 Hz QP that solves in about 12 ms. The tracker gets wider attitude and load bounds and a thrust margin compared with the planner. The ablation in that paper is the key evidence: the full aero model reached **0.62 m / 0.46 m/s**, a drag-only model 0.78 m, uncompensated lift 3.66 m, and an analytic tracking law **3.99 m / 2.48 m/s**, with aero-load violations. In Monte Carlo with ±15% C_D/C_L, 0-8 m/s wind, σ 100 m position and σ 15 m/s velocity, it succeeded in **476 of 495 runs (96.2%)**, with failures clustered at high wind combined with large aero error. An analytical dual-thrust law with adaptive ignition and aero correction converged in 1,000 runs with 50% aero error ([arXiv 2608.16000](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16000)). Blackmore defines SpaceX's targets as 99% landing ellipses of **10 m (drone ship) and 30 m (land)**, and warns that a suboptimal solution can exhaust propellant ([NAP ch.10](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/23659/chapter/10)).

**Inferences: diagnosis.** The latched law a_z = g + (v² − v_t²)/(2h) commits to an ignition point and a deceleration profile that assume the modeled drag. When the true drag is lower (scale 0.68), the vehicle arrives faster than predicted, the burn starts late, and the latch prevents recovery. The ZEM/ZEV lateral law is drag-free and gets its robustness only from feedback. The 94%→86% drop is the expected signature of this failure.

**Recommendations, in order of cost.** (1) *Unlatch and replan every step.* Recompute the required deceleration from the current state and the available thrust, and raise throttle as needed. (2) *Plan at 70-80% throttle, with drag at a conservative low bound* (for example k_drag = 0.6), so that drag shortfalls are absorbed by thrust margin. This mirrors MPG's planner/tracker margin split. The specific percentage is not sourced. (3) *Estimate drag online.* During the unpowered coast, compute k_drag = (a_meas − a_thrust − g)/(½ρv²SC_D,model/m), low-pass filter it, and feed it into both the ignition predictor and a drag feedforward term in ZEM/ZEV. That is the analytic analogue of MPG's drag-only case. (4) *Upgrade the G-FOLD mode* to iterate the SOCP 2-3 times with drag from the previous solution treated as a known time-varying acceleration. Then move to full SCvx with a trust region and free ignition time, closed in loop at 1-4 Hz, or as an MPG-style planner plus tracker. (5) *Benchmark the change fairly.* Rerun Monte Carlo with ±15% C_D about a corrected baseline (matching MPG) and again with ±30%, and report miss distance against the 30 m / 99% land-pad standard as well as success rate. This report found no primary source for Falcon 9 throttle reserve or online C_D estimation, so recommendations (2) and (3) are engineering judgment.

## The 67.5 t reserve is above the only RTLS estimate found

**Sourced facts, weakest section.** SpaceX publishes no reserve or Δv figures. Musk's 2013 estimate was a payload penalty of about 15% for drone-ship landings and about 30% for RTLS ([CS Monitor](https://csmonitor.com/Science/2016/0119/Why-does-SpaceX-keep-trying-to-land-rockets-on-floating-barges)). A 2018 forum compilation of unclear method gives about **56.3 t remaining at MECO for land landings** and about 27.1 t for drone-ship landings, out of a 409.5 t load ([New Mars forum](https://newmars.com/forums/viewtopic.php?id=7408)). Press-kit timelines put RTLS MECO at T+2:21-2:30, boostback start at T+2:39-2:42 and entry burn at about T+6:00-6:30 ([Spaceflight Now CRS-12](https://spaceflightnow.com/2017/08/13/timeline-for-spacexs-next-station-cargo-launch/); [CRS-16](https://spaceflightnow.com/2018/12/05/launch-timeline-for-spacexs-16th-resupply-launch-to-the-space-station/)). Blackmore notes there is rarely propellant for a second landing attempt ([Blackmore 2017](https://gwern.net/doc/technology/2017-blackmore.pdf)). Academic work treats MECO state as the design variable. PoliMi builds propellant-optimal RTLS envelopes across a range of MECO conditions ([MDPI Aerospace 11(1):35](https://www.mdpi.com/2226-4310/11/1/35)), and Kim et al. derive fuel-optimal convex boostback guidance, calling boostback the largest descent burn ([arXiv 2609.28759](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.28759)). The grid fins control lift and drag during reentry ([Smithsonian NASM](https://airandspace.si.edu/collection-objects/grid-fin-rocket-launch-vehicle-falcon-9/nasm_A20220607000)), but no credible L/D or crossrange figures were found. No quantitative study links wind to RTLS propellant use. Wind enters the literature as a guidance dispersion, modeled as an altitude-dependent random field ([arXiv 2504.11894](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.11894)).

**Inferences.** 67.5 t against about 56 t is a reserve roughly 20% too large. That is directionally consistent with Vyom's MECO arriving 6-10% early. Recalibrate to about **50-56 t nominal**, and label the forum source as low-pedigree. More importantly, replace the fixed number with an adaptive MECO criterion. During ascent, predict the recovery propellant from the current state and the wind profile, either with a fast boostback/entry/landing propagation or with a precomputed envelope over (MECO velocity, altitude, flight-path angle, headwind). Cut off when remaining propellant equals *nominal + k·σ*, with σ taken from Monte Carlo consumption. Payload dependence then falls out automatically: a heavier payload gives a slower MECO, which needs less boostback. Headwind starvation (wind blowing downrange, against the return) costs mostly in the subsonic descent and the landing burn. The cheapest mitigation is to aim the boostback upwind by the integrated predicted drift, so that grid-fin lift, which costs no propellant, absorbs it rather than the engine. Lofted boostbacks lengthen wind exposure, while flatter ones raise heating. Derive a propellant-per-(m/s headwind) sensitivity from Vyom's own Monte Carlo, because no published value exists.

## One hundred runs cannot demonstrate high reliability

**Sourced facts.** NASA MSFC practice states verification as "requirement met in at least y% of cases with x% consumer risk" ([Hanson & Beard, NASA/TP-2010-216447](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20100038453); [NTRS 20100035661](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100035661/downloads/20100035661.pdf)). For zero-failure demonstration, n = ⌈ln(1−C)/ln R⌉ ([arXiv 2303.03167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.03167)). Run counts in practice were 1,000 for X-33 ([NTRS 20010080460](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20010080460/downloads/20010080460.pdf)), 2,000 per landing site for MER ([Adler](https://marsoweb.nas.nasa.gov/landingsites/mer2003/doc/pasadena_01/talks/Adler-MER.pdf)), and 8,000 for MSL ([NTRS 20130011617](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20130011617/downloads/20130011617.pdf)). MER dispersed entry states, atmosphere and wind profiles, mass, and per-body drag coefficients ([NTRS 20040095913](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20040095913/downloads/20040095913.pdf)). SLS disperses each RS-25 independently and combines cluster thrust by root-sum-square ([NTRS 20190000734](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190000734/downloads/20190000734.pdf)). Earth-GRAM models wind as a mean plus correlated large- and small-scale perturbations, using Range Reference Atmosphere site data ([NTRS 20180006355](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180006355/downloads/20180006355.pdf); [NTRS 20180000878](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20180000878/downloads/20180000878.pdf)). FAA guidance also uses one-at-a-time 3σ sweeps ([FAA](https://www.faa.gov/sites/faa.gov/files/space/legislation_regulation_guidance/Attachment_1-Trajectory_Analysis.docx)). NASA-STD-7009A scores M&S credibility on eight factors from 0 to 4, with input pedigree scored at the lowest-quality input ([NTRS 20160011121](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20160011121/downloads/20160011121.pdf)).

**Inferences: arithmetic and recommendations.** With zero failures in 100 runs, the one-sided 90% lower bound is only **97.7%**. Demonstrating 99.73% at 90% confidence takes **852 failure-free runs**. Recommended changes: (1) Keep N=100 as a CI smoke test and run **≥2,000 cases** for release campaigns, logging a per-case seed so failures can be replayed. (2) Report one-sided Clopper-Pearson lower bounds alongside Wilson, and add percentiles of continuous margins (landing miss, residual propellant, max-q, heating), with the 99.865th percentile as one-sided 3σ. Margins converge far faster than rare-failure rates do. (3) Expand dispersions to cover per-body, per-regime aero multipliers (most urgently S1 entry and landing C_D, given the 0.68 finding), per-engine thrust, CG offset and inertia, thrust misalignment, actuator delay and rate limits, navigation errors, and separation-state errors. (4) Replace the scalar 5 m/s wind with altitude-correlated, GRAM-like wind profiles, and correlate density with them. (5) Add one-at-a-time 3σ sweeps to rank the dominant drivers. (6) Record a 7009-style pedigree for each dispersion in `run_manifest.py`. Numeric 1σ tables for aero, CG and misalignment were not retrieved. They should be pulled from the MER and InSight papers.

## Conclusion

Three of the four problems share one root cause: Vyom treats uncertain physics as if it were known. Roll damping uses the wrong geometry, landing guidance trusts its drag model, and the reserve is a single constant. The fixes also share a pattern. Model the physics per axis and per engine, estimate the quantities that drift in flight, and size margins statistically rather than by fiat. Order matters. Fix the J-matrix first, because it changes attitude behaviour that every later Monte Carlo depends on. Next, unlatch the landing burn and add the online drag estimate, which is cheap and aimed at the 8-point success loss. Then scale the Monte Carlo campaign so that the reserve can be re-derived as nominal + k·σ from Vyom's own data instead of a forum estimate.

The main remaining uncertainty is external validation. No primary source gives Falcon 9 reserve, throttle margin, roll inertia or touchdown statistics, so Vyom's calibration against "flight data" rests on webcast-derived timelines and community estimates. Webcast telemetry extraction is the most promising unexplored referent.
